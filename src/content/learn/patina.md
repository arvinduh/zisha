---
title: Raising the pot
hanzi: 养壶
---

Tea people say you *raise* a pot (养壶, yǎng hú), the way you'd raise a plant.
With steady use, the clay develops **bāojiāng** (包浆): a soft, warm, satin
glow that looks almost moist (温润, wēnrùn). It can't be bought or faked. It
only comes from tea and hot water over time.

### What builds patina

There's no extra ritual: brew in it often, and follow the
[routine after every session](/care/daily/#after) each time. Hot water and
tea, cycling in and out, are the only ingredients. Your pot's maker says to let
it air-dry rather than towel it; some guides polish with a cloth, but for this
pot, leave it be.

### What spoils it

- **Soap, cleaners, and fragrances.** They soak in and never fully leave.
- **Tea or wet leaves left inside.** These cause dark, uneven staining, a sour
  smell, and mold.
- **Grease.** Hand lotion, cooking oil, and kitchen haze make a sticky, glossy,
  muddy film. It looks shiny, but it's the opposite of patina: it clogs the
  clay.
- **Shortcuts.** Rubbing with spent leaves, or constantly pouring tea over the
  outside, gives blotchy color rather than a deep, even glow.

### How long it takes

It depends on the clay, and yours is the quickest. **Zhuni** takes on a
bright, glassy luster within weeks of regular use. It also shows fingerprints
and smudges, which is one more reason to handle it with clean, dry hands and
leave it alone while it dries. (Purple zini takes months; beige duanni, a year
or more.)

Don't chase it. A pot used gently a few times a week for a year will look
better than one rushed in a month.
