Bai Mudan comes from Fujian province in southeast China, mainly around Fuding
and Zhenghe. It's picked as a bud with its first one or two leaves. The name,
"white peony," comes from how the silvery buds and green leaves look together,
like a flower opening.

White tea is the least processed tea there is. The leaves are only withered
and dried: no rolling, no roasting. That's why it's so gentle and needs care
with heat. Expect honey, light florals, fresh hay, sometimes melon, and a soft,
sweet finish that lasts. On the usual grading ladder, Bai Mudan sits one step
below Silver Needle (all buds), and above Gong Mei and Shou Mei, which use more
mature leaves.

### Why it's in a zhuni pot

Zhuni is best known as an oolong clay. It's dense and iron-rich, so it holds
heat and absorbs very little, which also suits a delicate white tea: the pot
won't dull its aroma. The one thing to manage is that heat, which is why the
steps say 90 °C, short steeps, and the lid off between rounds. Every other tea
goes in the gaiwan.

### Aging

There's an old saying about white tea: *one year it's tea, three years it's
medicine, seven years it's treasure* (一年茶，三年药，七年宝). Aged white tea
grows darker, honeyed, and fruity. Tea people disagree about how well loose Bai
Mudan ages at home. If you're curious, set a small sealed portion aside and
taste it against fresh tea next year.

### Tuning a cup

- **Thin or watery?** A few more seconds, or half a gram more leaf.
- **Bitter or "cooked"?** Water at 90 °C, pour down the wall, drain every
  drop, and keep the lid off between steeps.
- **Flat, little aroma?** Warm the pot properly first, and check the tea has
  been kept sealed.
- **Fades by the tenth steep?** Lengthen the later steeps by the full 6 seconds
  each.
